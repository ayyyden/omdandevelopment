What to Know About Artificial Turf Installation Before You Start

From base preparation to infill selection, here's what every Southern California homeowner should understand before committing to an artificial turf installation.

By Omdan Development Inc — Licensed Contractor, License #1148568

Why Artificial Turf Makes Sense in the Desert

If you live anywhere from Palm Springs to Desert Hot Springs to Twentynine Palms, you already know the reality of trying to maintain a natural grass lawn: relentless sun, water restrictions, and monthly bills that climb all summer long. Artificial turf eliminates those pain points. It stays green year-round without irrigation, mowing, or fertilizer, and it holds up remarkably well in the extreme heat our region is known for.

Beyond the Coachella Valley, homeowners in Moreno Valley, Menifee, Murrieta, and Temecula are also making the switch. Water conservation is a priority across all of Southern California, and many local water agencies have offered rebate programs for turf removal in recent years. Swapping real grass for synthetic turf is one of the most practical upgrades a desert homeowner can make — but it's not as simple as rolling out a carpet. The installation process matters enormously, and cutting corners will cost you later.

Base Preparation Is the Most Critical Step

The single most important factor in a long-lasting artificial turf installation is what happens underneath the turf itself. A proper base typically involves excavating the existing soil or sod to a specific depth, compacting the subgrade, and then adding a layer of crushed aggregate — usually decomposed granite or Class II road base — that gets graded and compacted again. This creates a stable, well-draining surface that prevents the turf from shifting, wrinkling, or developing low spots over time.

In areas like Palm Desert and Yucca Valley, the native soil is often sandy and loose, which can be an advantage for drainage but a challenge for compaction. In other parts of the Inland Empire, particularly around San Bernardino, you may encounter more clay-heavy soil that drains poorly. A knowledgeable installer will assess your specific soil conditions and adjust the base preparation accordingly. Skipping this step or doing it halfway is the number-one reason turf installations fail prematurely.

If you're also considering hardscape elements around your turf area, the base prep needs to be coordinated carefully. For example, homeowners who want a paver border or walkway adjacent to turf should have both elements installed in a way that accounts for proper grading and drainage flow. Likewise, if you're integrating turf with a gravel or xeriscape design, the transitions between materials need to be planned from the start.

Drainage Matters More Than You Think

One of the most common misconceptions about artificial turf is that it doesn't need drainage planning because it's not real grass. The opposite is true. Rainfall in the Coachella Valley may be infrequent, but when it comes, it can come fast. Monsoon-season storms can dump significant water in a short period, and if your turf system doesn't drain properly, you'll end up with standing water, displaced infill, and potential damage to your base layer.

Quality artificial turf products are perforated with small drainage holes throughout the backing, allowing water to pass through and into the base layer below. From there, the compacted aggregate directs water away from the surface. The grading established during base prep is what ensures this water flows in the right direction — toward a drainage channel, a dry well, or simply toward the edges of your property. If your yard already has drainage challenges, your installer should address those before turf goes down.

For homes with adjacent gutter systems that direct roof runoff near the turf area, it's essential to make sure downspouts aren't dumping water directly onto the synthetic surface. Redirecting that flow is a simple fix that prevents a lot of headaches.

Choosing the Right Turf Product

Not all artificial turf is created equal, and the product you choose will affect everything from how your yard looks to how it feels underfoot and how long it lasts. Turf products vary in blade height, density, color, texture, and backing material. Shorter, denser turf tends to work well for putting greens and pet areas, while taller-blade options create a more natural lawn-like appearance.

For Southern California installations, you want a product that is UV-stabilized. The Coachella Valley sees some of the most intense sun exposure in the country, and a turf product that isn't rated for UV resistance will fade, become brittle, and break down far sooner than it should. Look for products that come with manufacturer warranties specifically covering UV degradation.

Color is another factor worth discussing. The best modern turf blends use multiple shades of green along with brown or tan thatch fibers woven into the base to replicate the look of real grass. Single-tone, bright green turf is immediately recognizable as fake and can detract from your home's curb appeal. Your installer should be able to show you samples and help you pick a product that complements your home's exterior. If you're also planning an exterior painting project, choosing your turf and paint palette together can create a cohesive, polished look.

Infill: What It Is and Why It Matters

Infill is the granular material spread across the turf after it's installed. It settles between the blades, weighing the turf down, supporting the fibers so they stand upright, and helping the surface feel more natural underfoot. Without infill, turf blades will flatten quickly, the material won't stay in place, and the overall look will suffer.

Several types of infill are available. Silica sand is one of the most common and affordable options. Rounded silica sand is generally preferred because it's less abrasive on the turf fibers. For pet owners, antimicrobial infill products are available that help reduce odor and bacteria. Some infill options are specifically designed to stay cooler in direct sunlight — a real consideration when surface temperatures in Palm Springs and surrounding cities can climb significantly during summer months.

Your installer should apply infill evenly across the entire surface and then brush the turf to distribute the granules and lift the blades. This step is what gives newly installed turf that full, lush appearance.

Seaming, Edges, and the Details That Show

When your turf area is larger than a single roll, seams are necessary. How those seams are handled separates a professional installation from a DIY attempt. Properly done, seams are glued and secured so they're virtually invisible. Poorly done seams will separate over time, creating visible lines, tripping hazards, and places where weeds can push through from below.

Edge treatment is equally important. Turf needs to be secured along its perimeter using bender board, concrete borders, or existing hardscape edges. In our work across the Inland Empire, we see a lot of turf installations that connect to concrete patios, pool decks, and driveways. Getting a clean, tight edge against those hard surfaces takes precision — and it's one of the first things people notice when they look at a finished project.

If you're installing turf around a pool, the approach needs special attention to both drainage and edge anchoring. Pool decks get wet regularly, and the transition between the deck surface and the turf must be watertight and secure. Homeowners considering a broader pool remodeling project often incorporate turf into the redesign, which allows everything to be planned and graded together for the best result.

Permits, HOAs, and Local Considerations

In most cases, a straightforward artificial turf installation in your front or backyard does not require a building permit in our service area. However, if the project involves significant grading changes, retaining walls, or electrical work for lighting, permits may be needed. It's always smart to check with your local building department — rules can vary between cities like Temecula, Murrieta, and unincorporated Riverside County areas.

If your home is in a community governed by a homeowners association, check your CC&Rs before starting any work. Many HOAs in the Coachella Valley and Inland Empire now allow artificial turf, but some have specific requirements regarding the product's appearance, pile height, or installation method. Getting HOA approval in writing before work begins saves you from potential conflicts after the project is done.
