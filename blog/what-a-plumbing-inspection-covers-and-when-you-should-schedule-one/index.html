What a Plumbing Inspection Covers and When You Should Schedule One

A thorough plumbing inspection can catch hidden leaks, aging pipes, and code violations before they turn into costly emergencies — here's what to expect and when to act.

By Omdan Development Inc — Licensed Contractor, License #1148568

Why Plumbing Inspections Matter for Desert Homeowners

Most homeowners don't think about the pipes behind their walls until something goes wrong — a spike in the water bill, a damp spot on the ceiling, or a slab leak that warps the flooring. In desert communities like Palm Springs, Palm Desert, and Desert Hot Springs, the combination of extreme heat, hard water, and shifting soils puts unusual stress on residential plumbing systems. A professional inspection is designed to catch trouble before it escalates.

Think of a plumbing inspection the same way you'd think of a vehicle tune-up. The goal isn't just to find problems; it's to understand the overall condition of the system and plan for maintenance that protects your investment. Whether you've owned your home for decades or you're closing on a property in Menifee or Temecula, an inspection gives you a clear picture of what's working and what needs attention.

What a Standard Plumbing Inspection Covers

A thorough plumbing inspection touches every part of the water supply and drainage system. Here's a breakdown of the key areas a qualified inspector will evaluate:

  • Supply lines and shut-off valves: The inspector checks for leaks, corrosion, and proper operation of the main shut-off valve and individual fixture valves.
  • Water pressure: Both excessively high and dangerously low pressure can signal problems in the supply line or the pressure regulator.
  • Water heater: The tank, connections, temperature and pressure relief valve, and anode rod are examined for wear, sediment buildup, and code compliance.
  • Drain and waste lines: Slow drains, gurgling sounds, and odors may indicate partial blockages or venting issues that need correction.
  • Fixtures and faucets: Every sink, toilet, shower, and bathtub is tested for leaks, proper drainage, and secure connections.
  • Visible pipes: Exposed piping in crawl spaces, utility rooms, and under sinks is inspected for corrosion, galvanic reactions between dissimilar metals, and outdated materials like polybutylene.
  • Sewer line condition: In many inspections, a camera scope is fed through the main sewer line to check for root intrusion, cracks, and bellied sections.

Some inspectors also evaluate outdoor plumbing, including hose bibs, irrigation connections, and pool supply lines. If you're planning a pool remodeling project, having the plumbing side assessed at the same time can save you from surprises mid-project.

Common Plumbing Issues in Southern California Homes

The Coachella Valley and Inland Empire share a few plumbing challenges that are less common in cooler, coastal climates. Hard water is one of the biggest culprits. Over time, mineral deposits narrow the interior diameter of pipes, restrict water flow, and corrode fixtures. If you've noticed white scale buildup around your faucets in Yucca Valley or Twentynine Palms, the same thing is likely happening inside your walls.

Slab leaks are another frequent issue, especially in older homes built on concrete foundations. Copper supply lines running beneath the slab can develop pinhole leaks due to soil chemistry and thermal expansion. Left unaddressed, a slab leak can damage your flooring, compromise your foundation, and send your water bill soaring.

Aging sewer lines also deserve attention. Many homes in San Bernardino and Moreno Valley were built with clay or cast-iron drain pipes that deteriorate over several decades. Tree root intrusion is common, and once a root finds its way into a joint, the blockage only gets worse. A camera inspection is the fastest way to diagnose the problem without digging up the yard.

When You Should Schedule a Plumbing Inspection

There are several situations where booking an inspection is more than just a good idea — it's essential. If you're buying a home, a plumbing-specific inspection goes deeper than the general home inspection and can reveal hidden deal-breakers. Properties that have been vacant for extended periods, which is common with seasonal residences in Palm Desert, are especially prone to dried-out traps, corroded valves, and critter damage to exposed lines.

Homeowners should also schedule an inspection before starting any major remodeling work. If you're planning a kitchen remodel or bathroom renovation, the plumber needs to verify that existing supply and drain lines can handle new fixtures, relocated sinks, or upgraded appliances like a dishwasher or pot filler. Discovering corroded galvanized pipe behind the wall after demolition is underway adds delays and costs that could have been avoided.

Other smart times to schedule an inspection include:

  • When your home is more than 20 years old and has never had a dedicated plumbing evaluation.
  • After a noticeable spike in your water bill with no change in usage.
  • When you detect musty smells, damp spots, or mold growth near walls and ceilings — issues that may also require drywall repair once the source is fixed.
  • Before converting a landscape to drought-tolerant gravel and xeriscape, since irrigation lines may need to be rerouted or capped.

What Happens After the Inspection

Once the inspection is complete, you'll receive a detailed report outlining the condition of each component. The report typically categorizes findings into immediate concerns — things like active leaks or code violations — and maintenance recommendations that can be addressed on a planned timeline. A good inspector will walk you through the findings and help you prioritize repairs.

For homeowners in Murrieta, Temecula, and throughout the Inland Empire, this report also becomes a valuable negotiating tool during a real estate transaction. Sellers can address problems proactively, and buyers can request repairs or credits before closing. Even if you aren't buying or selling, the report serves as a maintenance roadmap that helps you budget for future work.

When repairs involve fixtures, finishes, or surrounding surfaces, the work often overlaps with other trades. A leaking shower valve, for example, might require tile work to restore the wall after the plumber finishes. A corroded supply line under the kitchen sink could coincide with a countertop replacement you've been considering. Coordinating these projects together saves time, reduces disruption, and often delivers a better finished result.

How to Prepare for a Plumbing Inspection

You don't need to do much to get ready, but a few small steps help the inspection go smoothly. Clear out the area under every sink so the inspector has easy access to supply valves and drain connections. Make sure the water heater is accessible — move storage boxes or anything blocking the unit. If you have a crawl space, confirm the access hatch is unobstructed.

It's also helpful to jot down any symptoms you've noticed: slow drains, running toilets, discolored water, low pressure in specific fixtures, or water stains on walls and ceilings. These observations give the inspector a head start on where to look. And if you have documentation from previous repairs or remodeling work, share that as well. Knowing whether pipes were replaced or rerouted during a past bathroom remodel saves time and prevents redundant work.

Choosing the Right Professional for the Job

Not every plumber performs inspections, and not every inspector is a licensed plumber. For the most reliable results, look for a licensed professional who has experience with the specific construction styles and plumbing materials common in your area. Homes in Twentynine Palms may have very different plumbing configurations than mid-century properties in Palm Springs, and local knowledge matters.
